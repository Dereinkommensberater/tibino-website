Hide and seek for toddlers: from peekaboo to the real game

Hide and seek is one of the few games a child plays from infancy to primary school. What changes along the way is not the rules but what the child finds exciting about it.

Short answer. Start with peekaboo, then hide toys while your child watches, leaving a corner showing. Around two, children want to hide themselves — usually in plain sight, and that is fine. Real hide and seek, with counting and swapping roles, tends to work from three or four. At every age the rule is the same: the finding has to come, and soon.

Why the game starts so early

A baby laughing at peekaboo is celebrating a discovery: a face that disappears has not stopped existing. Psychologists call this object permanence, and it develops through the second half of the first year. Before it, a covered face is simply gone. After it, the covered face is thrilling, because it is about to come back.

Every later version of hide and seek rests on the same idea — that something out of sight still has a place, that you can look for it, and that waiting pays off.

What suits each age

  • From around six months: peekaboo with hands or a thin cloth. A second or two is plenty.
  • From around one: hide a toy under a cup or blanket while your child watches. Let part of it show at first.
  • Between two and three: your child hides themselves — behind the curtain, under the duvet, often in the same place as last time.
  • From about three or four: the real game, with counting, searching and taking turns. Now a child can bear not being found for a little while.

These ages are rough guides. Some children want peekaboo long after the book says they should have moved on; others are desperate to hide properly at two. Follow the laughter.

Why two-year-olds are terrible at hiding

Feet under the curtain, head under a cushion, the rest of the body in full view. Many small children simply cover their own eyes and are certain they cannot be seen. Research from the University of Cambridge in 2012 suggests why: young children seem to think of seeing as mutual — if our eyes do not meet, neither of us can see the other.

Play along. Look loudly in three wrong places — “Is she behind the sofa? No. In the kitchen? No” — before you find them. The giggling from the hiding place is the best part of the game, and it tells your child you were really looking.

A few rules that keep it fun

  • Always find them. A toddler left unfound too long gets frightened, not excited. Too early is better than too late.
  • Let sound help. The hider can hum or squeak quietly, which turns searching into listening.
  • Safe hiding places only. No washing machines, lidded chests or cupboards that latch from outside.
  • Swap roles. Children want both — to be found and to find. For the youngest, finding is often the bigger thrill.

On screen, the child should know more than the character

Hide and seek works best in a video when it is turned around: the viewer can see the hiding place and the character cannot. That puts the child in charge. They are not waiting to be told the answer — they already have it, and they get to point.

A quiet version for bedtime

Hide and seek does not have to be wild. Before sleep, try a soft version: a cuddly toy hides somewhere in the bedroom and your child looks for it while you whisper “colder” or “warmer”. Whoever is found gets a goodnight kiss and goes into bed. Three or four rounds, then stop — the ending is part of the game.

How much screen time fits

The American Academy of Pediatrics recommends avoiding screen media other than video chatting before about 18 to 24 months, and for children aged two to five, around an hour a day of high-quality programming, ideally watched together. A four-minute hide-and-seek episode sits comfortably inside that — but it does not replace the real thing, with a child giggling under a blanket and waiting to be found.

Frequently asked

At what age can a child play hide and seek?

Peekaboo works from around six months. Hiding and finding toys usually works from the first birthday. Children often want to hide themselves from about two, and the full game with counting and seeking tends to work from three or four.

Why does my toddler cover their eyes to hide?

Because they believe that if they cannot see you, you cannot see them. Young children appear to think of seeing as something mutual. It usually fades by about four — until then, it is mostly just very funny.

What if my child gets scared during hide and seek?

Find them faster and keep talking while you search, so they can hear your voice. Some children feel safer seeking together with an adult rather than hiding alone. It is also fine to leave the game for a while and come back to it.

Is peekaboo actually good for development?

It practises several things at once: remembering where something is, waiting, and learning that people who go away come back. Mostly, though, it is fun — and at this age that is reason enough.
