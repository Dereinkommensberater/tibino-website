Videos that help language development

Screens are usually discussed as a risk to language. They can be, but the picture is more specific than that — and the details are useful.

Short answer. Video alone teaches young children far less than a person does — the “video deficit”. What helps: an adult watching along and talking, slow clear speech, pauses that invite a response, repetition, and words tied to something visible on screen.

The video deficit, briefly

Children under about two and a half learn markedly less from a screen than from the same information delivered by a person in the room. This is a well-replicated finding known as the video deficit, and the gap narrows with age but does not disappear immediately.

The practical consequence is not “no video”. It is that video works best when it gives an adult something to talk about, rather than replacing the adult.

What makes a video more useful

  • Pauses. A question with a real silence after it invites the child to speak. This is the single most valuable feature.
  • Slow, clear speech. Fewer words, well separated, beats dense narration.
  • Words tied to pictures. The named object visible while it is named.
  • Repetition within the episode. The same word returning three times.
  • Real voices. Human speech with natural intonation, not synthesised delivery.

Formats that ask a question and wait — “who made that sound?” — do more for speech than formats that simply narrate, because the child produces language rather than receiving it.

What you do matters more than what is on

The strongest lever is not the programme. It is whether someone comments, names things and answers questions while it plays. That turns a one-way stream into something closer to a conversation, which is the format language is actually built in.

Three habits that cost nothing: name what is on screen, repeat what your child says and extend it slightly (“cow — a big brown cow”), and ask something after the episode rather than during it.

The first-words format, and what it asks of the child

There is a narrow kind of episode built entirely around the point above: one object on screen, its name said clearly, and then a direct request — can you say it? No story, no jokes, nothing else happening. It looks almost too plain to be worth three minutes, and for a child at the one-word stage it is often the most useful thing on the platform.

What makes it work is that the request is explicit. Most videos hope the child will join in; this kind asks. A two-year-old who would not spontaneously repeat a word will frequently answer a question addressed to them, because being asked is a social event rather than a broadcast.

The detail that separates a good one is whether the word comes back. A single naming is a coin toss — the child may have been looking away, or chewing, or halfway through a thought. A format that returns to the same animal a minute later, doing something different, gives the word a second and a third chance without feeling like a drill.

Our episode Zoo Animals for Toddlers is built to that rule. Nine animals, each met twice: Ellie the elephant lifts her trunk and later drinks with it, Penny the penguin waddles and later swims, Bramble the bear digs and later sits back and scratches his tummy. Every stop is three lines long — the discovery, one thing that makes the animal itself, and then the question — and then a pause.

Sit where your child can see your face during a video like this, and answer the question out loud yourself the first two or three times. Children repeat a word far more readily when a person in the room has just said it than when a screen has.

What to be careful with

Background television is worth reducing even when nobody is watching: it measurably reduces how much adults speak to children in the room, and adult speech is the main input for early vocabulary.

Very fast content is also a poor fit, because words arrive too quickly to be picked out. Slow content with fewer words gives a toddler more usable language than dense content with many.

Frequently asked

Can TV cause a speech delay?

Television does not cause speech delay by itself. Heavy screen use is associated with slower language growth, mainly because it displaces conversation. The amount of talking a child hears and does is the factor that matters most.

Do educational videos teach vocabulary?

They can, modestly, especially after about two and a half and when an adult watches along. Below that age children learn considerably less from screens than from people — the video deficit.

Are subtitles useful for toddlers?

Not for pre-readers. They can help older children who are beginning to read. For toddlers, clear speech and a visible object are what matter.

Is it better to watch in one language only?

Bilingual households do not need to restrict to one language. Consistency of source helps — one language per person or per context — but exposure to two languages is not a cause of delay.
